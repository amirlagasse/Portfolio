Screenless Wearable Project (In Progress)

Wearable Project Inspiration

During my senior year on the high school rowing team, a teammate showed me his new heart rate monitor: a sleek device with no screen. He walked me through the app that paired with it. As someone who is obsessed with analyzing my training data, I fell in love immediately. I signed up for a membership that night, and it arrived a few days later.
After about a month of use, I started noticing a whole array of problems. The first problem was that it wasn't accurate. Even with a bicep band, the device still had significant dropouts and frequently overestimated my heart rate during rowing. My second issue was compatibility. The app was challenging to extract data from and transfer to my workout hub, TrainingPeaks. Finally, my biggest issue was that the product didn't match its advertising. The idea was an all-in-one, distraction-free device that sits on your wrist, collects all your metrics, and lets you analyze them to live a healthier life. A truly distraction-free device wouldn't require me to have my phone with me when I wanted to track anything beyond an indoor workout. To get any pace or distance metrics, the device needs the user to start the workout and run with their phone to get any reliable data from it.

I sat on this for several years until a couple of other companies got into the screenless wearable game, such as Polar with its Polar Loop and Amazfit with its Helio Strap. All of their new devices changed the pricing model from a subscription to a one-time price, which was more appealing to consumers. However, the underlying sensors and performance remained unchanged. If no one was going to do it, I was going to take a stab at it.

Currently, I have developed a working sensor prototype using a Seeed Studio XIAO nRF52840 microcontroller paired with a MAX30102 heart rate and SpO₂ sensor. I chose the Seeed Studio because it is the smallest Arduino-compatible board on the market. Still, after initial testing, I will design a PCB to integrate all the components into a single, concise board.  To address the accuracy limitations of wrist-based photoplethysmography, I also plan to develop a mechanical adapter that can quickly unclip from the wrist and reattach to the chest, mimicking a traditional chest strap for higher-fidelity recordings during intense activity. The device streams real-time heart rate data over Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) in the standard Heart Rate Service format. I've also integrated GPS, IMU-based motion sensing, and vibration feedback for a full-featured, screenless experience that doesn't require the constant phone interaction that Whoop and these other companies' wearables require.

Beyond the hardware, I've also built a custom Flutter-based companion app (Git attached below) that successfully gathers and analyzes the data. It is still in development, but more features will be added as soon as the initial device testing concludes. The project is still evolving, but the goal remains the same: to build the wearable I always wished I had.

Custom Flutter Application

HR data gathered from the IR sensor, plus current coordinates from the GPS. HR data is processed and sent to the Flutter app via BLE.

This screen recording walks through the interface of my custom wearable app. It starts in the Settings tab, where users can pair a heart rate strap using the device selector. Once selected, the app remembers that device and automatically reconnects when the app is reopened. Still in Settings, users can assign color labels to different workout types. These labels carry over into charts and session summaries for easy scanning.

I also added a feature that lets users start workouts directly from the wearable. After setting colors for each workout in the app, the onboard LED displays the selected workout color during tap-based selection on the device. The user cycles through the options using haptic taps, and once the correct workout is highlighted, they confirm and begin the session without needing their phone.

The Workouts tab shows recent sessions using those same color labels. Tapping on a session opens a detail view with summary stats and charts. The Dashboard displays key health metrics, including current heart rate and connection status. The 24-hour heart rate chart uses a translucent red fill and clearly marks data gaps with dashed lines.

At the end of the video, I show the editable Dashboard layout. Pressing "Edit Dashboard" outlines each tile and opens a tray of available widgets at the bottom. You can drag widgets like HRV, SpO₂, and Steps onto the grid. Half-width tiles align into shared rows, and full-width charts drop into their own rows. Tiles can be x-ed out to remove them, and the layout is saved when the user selects "Done Editing."
